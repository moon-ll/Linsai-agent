Abstract 
Twisted bilayer systems host a wealth of emergent phenomena , such as flat -band 
superconductivity, ferromagnetism, and ferroelectricity, arising from moiré superlattices and 
unconventional interlayer coupling. Despite their central role, direct and quantitative access 
to the three-dimensional atomic arrangement in these systems has remained elusive due to 
their nanoscale dimensions. Here, we introduce an automated dark-field electron tomography 
technique that enables  quantitative three-dimensional structural analysis of atomically thin 
materials with sub-ångström precision.  By applying this method to twisted bilayer WSe₂, we 
precisely visualize the twist -angle-dependent structural relaxation appearing as the AB/BA 
stacking domains separated by 10-20 nm domain walls. In the marginally twisted region (𝜃 ≤ 
0.1°), we uncover a significant expansion of the interlayer spacing compared to the bulk 
configuration, exceeding 0.1 Å, along with a remarkable temperature -driven interlayer 
decoupling. Ultrafast measurements further reveal optically induced interlayer separation of 
~0.2 Å on the picosecond timescale, attributed to transient exciton formation. These findings 
not only establish a powerful approach for visualizing hidden out-of-plane structures in 
2 
 
atomically thin micro-flake materials, but also uncover the intrinsic fragility and dynamical 
tunability of interlayer coupling in moiré-engineered 2-dimensional materials.

 Twisted bilayers of van der Waals materials have opened new frontiers in condensed 
matter physics, offering a versatile platform for realizing strongly correlated, topological, 
and ferroelectric phases. When two atomically thin crystals are stacked with a slight 
rotational mismatch, long -wavelength m oiré superlattices emerge, reshaping the band 
structure and vibrational modes through periodic modulation of the local atomic registry. 
These engineered systems exhibit an extraordinary array of phenomena, including flat -band 
superconductivity [1–3], gate -tunable ferromagnetism  [4], and polar textures akin to 
ferroelectricity [5–7]. While graphene -based m oiré systems have led this revolution, 
semiconducting transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) provide an equally rich landscape, 
where twist angle, stacking sequence, and layer composition can be precisely controlled to 
manipulate superconductivity [8,9], correlated insulating states [10–12], moiré excitons [13], 
phonon modes [14], and charge -density waves [15]. As such, twisted bilayer TMDs have 
rapidly become a model system for probing and designing emergent quantum states. 
These phenomena originate from the three -dimensional (3D) atomic arrangement 
created when two atomically thin layers are twisted to form a moiré superlattice . In twisted 
bilayers, a small twist angle reorganizes the local atomic registry both in -plane and out-of-
plane, giving rise to reconstructed AB/BA domains, domain walls, and a spatially modulated 
interlayer geometry across the moiré unit cell   [16,17], as shown in Fig. 1(a,b) . This 
reconstructed 3D structure strongly modifies the interlayer coupling, which in turn governs 
a wide range of emergent properties: interlayer hybridization underpins the formation of flat 
bands [1,2,10], out -of-plane polarization couples with in -plane shear to enable sliding 
ferroelectricity [5–7], and excitonic properties can be tuned by controlling the interlayer 
distance to favor intralayer or interlayer excitons  [18]. Therefore, understanding moiré 
materials requires quantitative knowledge of both in -plane and out -of-plane structural 
degrees of freedom.  However, these two aspects have so far been accessed in a highly 
unbalanced manner: in-plane structures have been extensively imaged using  piezoresponse 
force microscopy,  scanning tunneling microscop y (STM), and transmission electron 
microscopy (TEM), whereas quantitative measurements of out-of-plane (stacking-direction) 
structure have proven exceptionally challenging. Atomic force microscopy and theoretical 
4 
 
modeling suggest interlayer spacing varia tions of 0.1 –0.8 Å across the m oiré 
superlattice [17,19–24], but three -dimensional reconstruction of  the interlayer geometry 
remains elusive, posing a significant barrier to  understanding and engineering moiré-driven 
phenomena. 
In principle, diffraction measurements provide a route to extracting 3D structural 
information from two -dimensional (2D) materials, just as in conventional crystals. As an 
illustrative example, Fig. 1 (c) shows a dark -field TEM image of the 0 2 diffraction from 
twisted bilayer WSe₂ , revealing triangular AB/BA domains via intensity contrast. This 
contrast arises from the scattering-vector [𝒒 = (𝑞𝑥, 𝑞𝑦, 𝑞𝑧)] dependence of the diffraction 
intensity 𝑆(𝒒), which differs between AB and BA stacking due to their opposing rod -like 
scattering features along 𝑞𝑧. The 𝑞𝑧-resolved intensity encodes structural parameters such as 
the selenium height (h) and interlayer spacing (d), offering a pathway to quantify the out-of-
plane structure from tilt -dependent diffraction patterns. However, conventional x -ray 
diffraction is not viable for atomically thin micro -flake samples. While positron diffraction 
has succeeded in millimeter-scale bilayer graphene [25], its applicability remains limited for 
micro-flakes as well. In contrast, TEM offers a compelling solution. As well as the sufficient 
spatial resolution that can access the AB/BA stacking domain structure, recent advances in 
automated 3D electron diffraction tomography enable high-precision refinement of 3D 
structural models  [26,27] based on the diffraction intensity . Similar principles can be 
extended to atomically thin materials using tilt -dependent dark -field imaging  [28], and 
further integrated with ultrafast TEM to resolve structural dynamics on picosecond 
timescales. These emerging techniques provide a powerful foundation for probing the elusive 
3D crystal structure and its dynamics in 2D materials. 
In this study, we introduce a method we term “automated dark -field tomography”, 
which enables quantitative reconstruction of the 3D structure of twisted bilayer systems from 
tilt-series dark -field TEM images. Using this approach, we first reveal the twist -angle-
dependent structural relaxation, showing the evolution from fully-relaxed AB/BA domains 
in marginally twisted regions to incompletely relaxed structures at larger twist angles. We 
5 
 
then directly determine the interlayer spacing and chalcogen atom height in both twisted and 
naturally stacked bilayer WSe₂, revealing that both are significantly expanded compared to 
the bulk 2H-WSe₂ phase. Strikingly, we find that fully relaxed AB/BA domains of twisted 
bilayer WSe₂ exhibit sub -ångström interlayer decoupling upon heating, an effect absent in 
naturally stacked counterparts. Moreover, by integrating ultrafast TEM techniques  [29,30], 
we uncover a light -induced, nonthermal expansion of the interlayer spacing on the 
picosecond timescale, with magnitudes exceeding those  in bulk materials. These results 
establish a powerful new methodology for structural analysis of 2D materials and reveal the 
exceptional fragility and tunability of interlayer coupling in twisted bilayers, driven by both 
thermal and optical stimuli.

 𝐼02(02̅)(𝒒) can be obtained via the α-dependent dark-field intensities. The triangular contrast

Twist-angle dependent relaxation of crystal structure 
Here, we investigate how the crystal structure relaxation evolves as a function of twist 
angle 𝜃 using automated dark -field tomography. Although the target twist angle during 
sample preparation was 0.3°, we observe a range of domain sizes at different positions within 
the same flake due to the extrinsic inhomogeneity. Fig. 2(a) shows the dark -field intensity 
𝐼02(𝛼) curves taken within the AB/BA stacking domains and at the domain wall under 
marginally twisted (𝜃 <0.05°) conditions. As described above, 𝐼02(𝛼) exhibits distinct peak 
structures depending on the local stacking configurations. As a result, the dark-field images 
at 𝛼 = 24° [Fig. 2(b-d)] visualize the stacking domains. To qualitatively evaluate the local 
stacking configurations, we focused on the angular shift of the peak in 𝐼02(𝛼) between 0° 
and 10°. This peak in Fig. 2(a) monotonically changes among the AB domain, BA domain, 
and domain wall regions, suggesting that its relative position is sensitive to the local stacking 
configuration across the domain wall. Based on this observation, we use the relative spatial 
variation of the center of mass (CoM) between 0° and 10° [Fig. 2(e-g)] to visualize shifts in 
the 𝐼02(𝛼) peak position associated with local stacking configurations. It should be noted that 
the absolute CoM value appears as “angle” but cannot be directly assigned to any structural 
parameter, as the peak is very broad and the CoM depends on the selected angular window. 
CoM in Fig. 2(e,f) clearly visualizes the domain walls (appearing as yellow in the CoM 
7 
 
colorscale) as well as the fully relaxed AB/BA domain regions (brown/light green). Here we 
note that the present spatial resolution is better than the pixel size (6.32 nm). The CoM profile 
in Fig. 2(h,i) exhibits a clear step -like change between the AB and BA domains, reflecting 
the presence of a well-defined domain wall between fully relaxed AB/BA domains. The full 
width of this step is approximately 15 nm , which is comparable to that reported for bilayer 
graphene (~10 nm) [32]. The CoM becomes nearly flat within about 10 nm away from the 
domain wall suggesting that the AB/BA stacking relaxation is essentially complete over most 
of the domain area. At a larger twist angle of 𝜃 =0.27° in Fig. 2(g), the contrast is lower than 
that at smaller twist angles, implying incomplete relaxation in the AB/BA stacking regions. 
The CoM profile in Fig. 2(j) no longer shows a step -like feature separated by flat AB/BA 
regions, but instead exhibits a more gradual variation across the moiré period. This indicates 
that the stacking configuration is continuously modulated over the entire  region, rather than 
being fully relaxed into AB/BA domains separated by narrow domain walls. Notably, this 
incomplete relaxation occurs even though the moiré period is still about five times larger than 
the domain-wall width observed at smaller twist angles. 
The present results reveal a distinct feature of domain walls in twisted-bilayer systems. 
Previous studies have shown that AB/BA stacking reconstruction becomes significant below 
about 1 ° in both twisted -bilayer graphene and transition metal dichalcogenides  [16,17], 
which is consistent with the clear AB/BA domain contrast of the dark -field images in Fig. 
2(b-d). Nonetheless, the present results show that  deviation from the fully relaxed AB/BA 
stacking configuration already exists across nearly the entire region even at a relatively small 
angle (𝜃 =0.27°) due to the intrinsic strain caused by twisted structure, a point that has not 
been fully discussed in previous studies. In contrast, we found nearly homogeneous AB/BA 
stacking when 𝜃 ≤ 0.10 ° , probably because the domain size is much larger than the 
characteristic domain-wall width.  These results indicate that, at twist angles below 0.10°, 
most of the AB/BA domain region is nearly fully relaxed into AB/BA stacking configurations 
while the local structure remains incompletely relaxed over nearly the entire domain area  at 
θ = 0.27°.

Crystal structure refinement of twisted- and natural-bilayer WSe2 
Here, we experimentally determine the interlayer distance of twisted - and natural -
bilayer WSe2 using automated dark-field tomography. We focus on the twist angle 𝜃 ≤ 0.10° 
because our structural refinement is based on the assumption that the local crystal structure 
is fully relaxed to AB/BA stacking. Figure 3(a) shows the dark-field intensity 𝐼02(𝛼) curves 
taken within the AB/BA stacking domains of 𝜃 = 0.10°, as marked by the blue/red rectangles 
in the inset image. We fitted them by the squared structure factor  |𝑆02(𝛼, 𝑑TBL, ℎTBL)|2 to 
determine 𝑑TBL and ℎTBL [see Sec. 1 in Supplemental Materials  for details]. We assumed 
that the selenium height ℎTBL is identical for all selenium atoms for simplicity. The solid gray 
curves in Fig. 3(a) are the fitting results that most well reproduce the experimental data. Here 
we obtained the structural parameters as 𝑑TBL = 6.64 ±  0.10 Å and ℎTBL = 1.76 ±  0.10 
Å. We also measured the natural-bilayer 2 H-WSe2, as shown in the inset in Fig. 3(b). 
Similarly to the twisted-bilayer sample, 𝐼02(𝛼) of the bilayer 2H-WSe2 was taken at the blue 
rectangle region (inset) and fitted by the gray solid curve. 𝑑2𝐻 and ℎ2𝐻 are obtained as 6.63 
± 0.10 Å and 1.77 ± 0.10 Å, respectively. 
The results of crystal structure refinements  for twisted- and natural- bilayer WSe2 are 
summarized in Fig. 3(c), along with the literature values for bulk 2H-WSe₂ [33]. The results 
of structural relaxation from density-functional theory (DFT) calculations are also plotted by 
filled markers  (see methods) . Compared with bulk 2H -WSe2, both twisted - and natural -
bilayer WSe2 show a significantly large interlayer distance d, although the selenium height h 
is seemingly scattered within the experimental error. The larger interlayer distance observed 
in both bilayer systems suggests a reduced interlayer coupling, which should significantly 
affect their electronic and optical properties, as discussed later. The DFT calculation results 
(filled markers) also show the significant difference between the interlayer distance of bulk 
and bilayer samples , well reproducing the experimental results.  This result gives the 
validation of the DFT calculations in twisted-bilayer systems, at least in the relaxed AB/BA 
regions.

Thermally-induced sub-Å interlayer decoupling  
We further perform the temperature-dependent automated dark -field tomography  of 
natural- and twisted-bilayer WSe2, and reveal the thermally induced sub-Å interlayer 
decoupling in the twisted-bilayer sample. Fig. 3(d) shows the temperature-dependent 𝐼02(𝛼) 
curves for 24° < 𝛼 < 35°, taken at the BA stacking region of 𝜃 = 0.10°. At 400 K, the peak 
position is slightly shifted towards the lower 𝛼 direction compared to 300 K. On the other 
hand, 𝐼02(𝛼) for the natural-bilayer sample [Fig. 3(e)] does not show any significant shift. 
Similar behaviors were also observed in the AB stacking counterpart, other 𝛼 region, and 
marginally twisted regions (𝜃 < 0.05°) [Sec. 2 in Supplemental Materials]. Correspondingly, 
the fitting results of the twisted-bilayer sample show the change in the interlayer distance  
Δ𝑑TBL = 𝑑TBL(𝑇 = 400 K) − 𝑑TBL(𝑇 = 300 K)  of 0.08 Å  [Fig. 3(f)], whereas no 
significant change is observed in the natural-bilayer sample [Fig. 3(g)]. The estimated linear 
thermal expansion coefficient  along z for the twisted -bilayer sample is 𝛽 = 8.0 × 10 -5 K-1, 
which is much larger than those of the natural-bilayer sample (< 2.0 × 10-5 K-1) and the bulk 
2H-WSe2 sample [34] (1.0 × 10-5 K-1) as summarized in Fig. 3(h). This value is comparable 
to polymers such as polyvinyl chloride,  showing larger thermal expansion compared to 
inorganic solids [35].  These results reveal an anomalously large thermally induced sub -Å 
interlayer decoupling in fully relaxed AB/BA domains of twisted bilayer WSe₂, far exceeding 
that of the natural 2H bilayer. However, since the present comparison is effectively made 
between a 3R -like twisted region and a natural 2H bilayer, the current experiment alone 
cannot determine whether this behavior sh ould be attributed to the 3R -stack-like local 
stacking itself or to the twisted bilayer prepared by tear-and-stack method. Direct comparison 
with natural 3R and 2H bilayers, for example in MoS₂, will be an important direction for 
future work because no bulk 3R -WSe₂ crystal has been reported from which an equivalent 
reference bilayer could be prepared. 
This anomalously large thermal expansion  and sub-Å interlayer decoupling observed 
exclusively in the twisted -bilayer sample may originate from its unique bonding 
configuration associated with the catenary -like interlayer state. In twisted bilayers, the 
10 
 
interlayer coupling is highly nonuniform across the moiré superlattice: theoretical 
studies [23,17,20] have predicted a substantial difference in the interlayer spacing between 
the AA stacking regions, where the layers are maximally separated at the vertices of the 
triangular pattern, and the AB/BA stacking regions, where the layers are most closely spaced, 
with a magnitude on the order of several tenths of an ångström.  A recent STM study also 
reveals the out-of-plane atomic reconstruction depending on the stacking  [36]. The present 
results, obtained from the structurally relaxed AB/BA regions, already indicate an unusually 
strong anharmonicity in the interlayer bonding potential of the twisted-bilayer system. Such 
enhanced anharmonicity may naturally lead to a large out -of-plane thermal response, far 
exceeding that of natural bilayers and bulk crystals. Although the current analysis can be 
applied to only fully relaxed AB/BA regions, further improvement of the structure model 
will enable us to access local crystal structure for larger twist angle s, AA regions and the 
ridge-like domain boundaries of the moiré lattice. Probing these local structures is expected 
to provide decisive insights into how structural relaxation, anharmonicity, and thermally 
induced interlayer decoupling evolve with moiré geometry in the future. 
 
Non-thermal interlayer decoupling induced by pulsed light 
We further investigate the photoinduced structural dynamics of twisted -bilayer WSe2 
(Sample #2) using ultrafast TEM [Fig. 4(a)]. Sample #2 is characterized  in Sec. 3 in 
Supplemental Materials. Figure 4(b) shows the 0 2 dark-field image 𝐼02(𝑡) of twisted-bilayer 
WSe2 before photoexcitation , where triangular patterns are observed . Although the se 
reconstructed AB/BA stacking domains in Fig. 4(b) were stable under equilibrium conditions 
for more than a month, they change abruptly and irreversibly when the sample is continuously 
photoexcited by a sequence of 25 kHz 2.4 eV light pulses for several days [see Fig. 4(c) and 
Sec. 3 in Supplemental Materials]. The AB/BA domains at the center of the image lose the 
ordered triangular shape, but after one week, the structura l AB/BA domain shown in Fig. 
4(c) was stable with a marginal twist angle of less than 0.05°.

Here, we investigate the crystal structure dynamics of such marginally twisted WSe2 
from the time -dependent change in dark -field image intensity. Figure 4(d) shows the 0 2 
dark-field image of twisted-bilayer WSe2 measured at α =24.1°. When the 2.4 eV light pulse 
of 0.33 mJ/cm 2 excites the sample, the dark-field-image intensity in the AB (BA) stacking 
region decreases (increase s), as shown in Figs. 4(e,f). We found that triangular AB/BA 
domains also show a similar trend, as in Fig. S12 in Supplemental Materials, suggesting that 
the observed domain -dependent change in dark -field images is universal regardless of the 
domain shape . To quantitative ly analyze the dark-field intensity, we subtracted the 
background and obtained the normalized dark-field intensity  𝐼̅02(𝑡) for AB/BA domains as 
shown in Fig. 4(g) (see Sec. 4 in Supplemental Materials for details). 𝐼̅02(𝑡) of the AB (BA) 
domain decreases (increases) immediately after the photoexcitation, within the time 
resolution 4 ps, and then relaxes back. 
From the transient dark field intensity change, we further evaluate the time dependence 
of the interlayer distance  𝑑TBL(𝑡), revealing a sub-Å change in the interlayer distance. By 
comparing 𝐼02(𝑡)  and the simulated crystal structure factor |𝑆02(𝛼, 𝑑TBL, ℎTBL)|2 
quantitatively, we can determine 𝑑TBL(𝑡). For BA stacking, the effect of ℎTBL(𝑡) on 𝐼02(𝑡) 
is negligible, and we can only evaluate 𝑑TBL(𝑡) (see Sec. 5 in Supplemental Material).  As 
shown in Fig. 5(a), the obtained Δ𝑑TBL(𝑡) = 𝑑TBL(𝑡) − 𝑑TBL(𝑡 < 0) increases by 0.2 Å at t 
~ 0 and subsequently relaxes. Regarding the AB stacking, on the other hand, ℎTBL and 𝑑TBL 
both contribute to 𝐼02(𝑡)  and they cannot be independently evaluated. Here we a dopt 
ΔℎTBL(𝑡) = ℎTBL(𝑡) − ℎTBL(𝑡 < 0) = 0, which provides the  solution of  Δ𝑑TBL(𝑡) similar 
to the BA stacking (see Sec. 5 in Supplemental Material).  
As shown in Fig. 5(a), in the relaxation process, Δ𝑑TBL apparently shows the fast (about 
50 ps) and slow (> 200 ps) relaxation components. To discuss the origin of these relaxation 
components, we evaluated the change in the transient lattice temperature 𝛥𝑇 from the Debye-
Waller factor of 4 0 diffraction intensity, which is further normalized by that just after the 
photo-excitation ( 𝛥𝑇max) (see Sec. 5 in Supplemental Materials ). The 𝛥𝑇/𝛥𝑇max curve in 
Fig. 5(b) behaves differently from Δ𝑑TBL: an exponential decay curve with a single time 
constant of 200 ps can well describe 𝛥𝑇/𝛥𝑇max curve between 0 and 400 ps. These results 
12 
 
indicate that the photoinduced change in the interlayer distance cannot be attributed to the 
change in the transient lattice temperature and should be non -thermal, at least in the fast (< 
50 ps) timescale. 
The appearance of fast and slow decay components in the interlayer distance  is 
reminiscent of exciton dynamics observed in monolayer samples [37–40]. When the exciton 
density is sufficiently high in these systems, relatively fast (< 100 ps) decay of excitons due 
to the exciton-exciton annihilation process has been reported in addition to the slow (100 ps 
~ few ns) recombination process.  Such an exciton -exciton annihilation process is not 
observed in bulk and, therefore, is unique to atomically thin samples  [39]. These previous 
studies indicate that the non -thermal interlayer decoupling  observed in Fig. 5(a) may be 
attributed to the positive stress due to the exciton generation that is located at around 1.5-1.7 
eV in twisted-bilayer WSe2 [41]. The stress caused by the semiconductor carriers, including 
excitons, is usually described by deformation potential, which is related to the pressure -
dependent electronic band structure  [42]. Considering that the hydrostatic pressure 
significantly affects the interlayer coupling and bandgap in the bilayer system  [43], the 
deformation potential mechanism caused by the exciton is a plausible scenario for the change 
in the interlayer distance. In addition, we also mention that the  Δ𝑑TBL at 400 ps [Fig. 5(a)] 
is not fully relaxed to the initial state while 𝛥𝑇/𝛥𝑇max is almost zero [Fig. 5(b)]. This 
indicates the relatively slow ( t > 400 ps) exciton recombination process, in addition to the 
fast exciton-exciton annihilation (< 50 ps) and thermal relaxation (~ 200 ps)  as summarized 
in Fig. 5(c). To further verify the exciton-induced interlayer -decoupling scenario,  future 
measurements with varying photon energy and fluence would be valuable.  In particular, 
photon-energy dependence could help identify which excitonic states contribute to the 
interlayer decoupling. 
The observed 0.2 Å change in interlayer distance is significantly larger as compared to 
previous studies on various bulk materials [43,44]. In bulk graphite  [43], the magnitude of 
the change in interlayer distance is less than 0.01 Å even at a pump fluence of 11 mJ/cm 2, a 
value significantly different from the present result (0.2 Å at 0.33 mJ/cm2). Δ𝑑TBL = 0.2 Å is 
comparable to the 25 -50% interlayer distance variation between AA and AB/BA stacking 
13 
 
predicted by the theoretical calculations  [23,17,20]. Although we do not observe any 
reversible change in the AB/BA stacking domain shape on the picosecond scale, frequent 
repetition of such a giant change in the interlayer distance may induce the deformation of the 
local twist angle and the resultant disordered domain structures, as seen in Fig. 4(b,c). Such 
a photoinduced domain reconstruction should be further investigated in future studies. 
 
Discussion: Impact of sub-Å interlayer decoupling 
The observed thermally and optically induced sub-Å interlayer decoupling  will 
significantly impact  various properties  of twisted materials. The previous experiments 
reported that the properties of twisted-bilayer systems are significantly modified via a change 
in the interlayer distance , e.g., t uning of superconductivity  [45], change in the indirect 
bandgap [20], formation of commensurate stacking  [46], and modification of the electronic 
van Hove singularities  [24]. In addition, m any theoretical calculations  [20,19,21,24,47,48] 
have revealed the band structure modification  due to the change in the interlayer distance 
because it can directly affect the interlayer coupling. Based on the theoretical calculation in 
Ref. [20], Δ𝑑TBL = 0.2 Å changes the indirect bandgap of MoS2 by about 100 meV, although 
the direct bandgap at the K point is not significantly modified [20]. Such a large modification 
of the bandgap may induce direct -indirect bandgap transition in twisted-bilayer TMD 
semiconductors. Therefore, the thermally and optically induced sub-Å interlayer decoupling 
revealed by the present work should be essential in interpreting future research on twisted-
bilayer systems. In particular, an interlayer decoupling effect caused by the excitons should 
play an essential role in the optical nature of the twisted-bilayer system.  
 
Conclusions 
In conclusion, we  investigated the thermally and optically induced  interlayer 
decoupling in twisted-bilayer WSe 2 using TEM. A newly developed  automated dark-field 
tomography enables visualization of the local stacking configurations , including the 
boundary regions, and reveals that full relaxation into AB/BA stacking occurs only when the 
domain size is at least about one order larger than the domain -wall width. This technique 
14 
 
further quantitatively determines the three -dimensional crystal structure of twisted - and 
natural-bilayer WSe 2, revealing  that both twisted - and natural -bilayer samples show  
interlayer decoupling by 0.15 Å  compared to bulk samples. Additional thermally-induced 
interlayer decoupling is also observed only in the twisted-bilayer sample, which is 
significantly larger than  the bulk and natural -bilayer samples . Further investigation of 
picosecond crystal structure dynamics after photoexcitation revealed a non-thermal interlayer 
decoupling reaching 0.2 Å, probably due to the exciton generation . Such sub-Å interlayer 
decoupling in equilibrium and non -equilibrium states  should significantly impact  the 
electronic and optical properties of twisted -bilayer materials , and should be carefully 
considered in future studies. Application of the TEM-based methods presented in this study 
to a variety of materials/phenomena, such as sliding ferroelectricity  [5–7], moiré enhanced 
charge-density wave [15], and light-induced ferromagnetism [49], is a promising direction in 
the future.

Methods 
 
Setup of (ultrafast) transmission electron microscopy 
Transmission electron microscopy measurements were performed using a previously 
described system  [29] based on Tecnai Femto (Thermo Fisher Scientific) . The 150 -μm 
condenser aperture was used for all measurements. We used a 20 -μm objective aperture for 
bright-field and dark-field measurements unless otherwise denoted. A selected-area aperture 
of 200  μ m was used for the diffraction measurements.  For the automated dark -field 
tomography measurements, sample -tilt-dependent dark -field images are automatically 
collected with an angle step of 0.5 °. For the time-resolved measurements in Fig. 4,  we use 
1030-nm light source PHAROS (Light Conversion) with  the repetition rate of 25 kHz. We 
used frequency-doubled 514-nm light with a fluence of 330  μJ/cm2 as the pump light. The 
total time resolution was set at 4  ps for data acquisition to maintain a good signal -to-noise 
ratio throughout the entire measurement, although the best time resolution of our ultrafast 
transmission electron microscopy system was 750 fs.  The beam diameter of the pump light 
(~160 m) was set much larger than that of the sample (< 10 m). The spatial resolution of 
the dark -field imaging  was measured using a 0 2 dark -field image at bilayer -monolayer 
boundary region under the same experimental conditions as those used in Fig. 2. As shown 
in Fig. S13, the dark -field intensity is changed within one pixel of the image . This result 
indicates that the present measurements achieve a spatial resolution better than the pixel size 
(6.32 nm). 
 
Sample preparation 
The twisted -bilayer WSe 2 and few-layer 2H -WSe2 samples were fabricated by the 
mechanical exfoliation of bulk 2H -WSe2 (2D Semiconductors) and dry-transfer method 
using Elvacite 2552C (Lucite International Inc.) as a polymer stamp  [50]. The twist angles 
were set to 0.3° by tear-and-stack method [51], although the actual twist angle slightly varies 
around this value depending on the position. T he WSe2 flakes were sandwiched between a 
few layers of hexagonal boron nitride (hBN) and then placed on an amorphous silicon nitride 
22 
 
(SiNx) substrate (Norcada Inc.). We prepared two twisted-bilayer WSe2 samples (Sample #1 
and #2), which are used for temperature -dependent and time -resolved measurements, 
respectively. We use 20-nm and 100-nm SiNx substrate for Sample #1 and #2, respectively. 
The local twist angle 𝜃 of twisted-bilayer WSe2 is determined from the domain size L using 
the relation 𝐿 = 𝑎/[2sin(𝜃/2)], where 𝑎 = 3.32 Å is lattice constant. 
 
Drift correction of time-dependent data 
 For the time-dependent dark-field image measurement in Fig. 4, we performed time-

the signal -to-noise ratio. Before the summation, the sample drift between several 
measurements was corrected. We numerically evaluated the cross -correlation of dark -field 
images as follows:

𝑝,𝑞=−𝑁/4

where 𝐼𝑡,𝑛(𝑥𝑝, 𝑦𝑞) is the dark-field image at delay time t of n-th measurement, 𝐼ref(𝑥𝑝, 𝑦𝑞) is 
the dark-field image at t = -100 ps of the first measurement, 𝑥𝑖,𝑝, 𝑦𝑗,𝑞 represents the pixelated

the maximum position of 𝐴𝑡,𝑛(𝑥𝑖, 𝑦𝑗), by which the respective image was shifted. The typical 
magnitude of the shift was 0-4 pixels. All analyses were done using multi -dimensional data 
analysis and visualization platform lys [52]. 
 
Density-functional calculations 
 Density-functional theory (DFT) calculations  using VASP package  [53] were 
performed to evaluate the structural parameters of AB and 2H stacking bilayer WSe 2 and 
bulk 2H-WSe2. It is noted that we do not include the effect of twisted crystal structure for 
AB stacking bilayer WSe2. The calculations were performed in the framework of the van der 
Waals density functional theory [54] using the pseudo-potential method in conjunction with 
23 
 
the generalized gradient approximation. The wave functions were expanded in the plane 
wave basis with the cutoff energy of 500 eV to ensure good convergence of the stress and 
elastic tensor. The k-point sampling was set to 9 × 9 × 4 in the first Brillouin zone.  The a 
axis length was fixed to 3.32 Å, which is experimentally determined by the electron 
diffraction measurement. The interlayer distance and selenium height  were relaxed with a 
force convergence of 0.001 5 eV/Å. Although the two selenium heights are relaxed 
independently, the difference between them are less than 0.001 Å.
